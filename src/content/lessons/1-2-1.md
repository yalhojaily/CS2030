## Why engineers need logic

Every `if` statement, every database query and every test assertion is a logical formula. When a specification says "every request gets a response within 2 seconds unless the server is in maintenance mode", you need to know exactly which situations violate it. When a code reviewer says "this loop is correct", the only honest meaning is that there is an argument, a proof, that it does what it claims for **every** input, not just the ones you tried.

This lesson gives you two tools. The first is a precise language: propositions, connectives and quantifiers. The second is a set of proof techniques that let you establish facts about infinitely many cases with a finite argument. Book of Proof (Hammack) and Mathematics for Computer Science (Lehman, Leighton and Meyer) open with exactly this material, for the same reason.

## Propositions and connectives

<div class="box def">

<span class="tag">Definition</span>

A **proposition** (or statement) is a sentence that is either true or false, but not both.

</div>

"7 is prime" is a proposition (true). "$2 + 2 = 5$" is a proposition (false). "$x > 3$" is **not** a proposition, because its truth depends on $x$; it is an *open sentence*, and we will turn such sentences into propositions with quantifiers later. "Close the door" is not a proposition at all.

We build compound propositions from simple ones with **connectives**. Let $p$ and $q$ be propositions.

| $p$ | $q$ | $\lnot p$ | $p \land q$ | $p \lor q$ | $p \to q$ | $p \leftrightarrow q$ |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| T | T | F | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | F | T | F | F |
| F | T | T | F | T | T | F |
| F | F | T | F | F | T | T |

Two entries surprise almost everyone the first time.

**"Or" is inclusive.** $p \lor q$ is true when both are true. If you mean "one or the other but not both", that is a different connective, *exclusive or*, written $p \oplus q$.

**Implication is false in exactly one row.** $p \to q$ ("if $p$ then $q$") is false only when $p$ is true and $q$ is false. Think of it as a promise. "If you finish the project, I will pay you" is broken only if you finish and are not paid. If you do not finish, the promise is not broken, whatever happens, so the implication counts as true. This is called **vacuous truth**, and it is why the statement "every element of the empty list is negative" is true.

<div class="box warn">

<span class="tag">Common mistake</span>

Implication is not causation. "If $2+2=4$ then Riyadh is in Saudi Arabia" is true, because both parts are true, even though one has nothing to do with the other. Logic only tracks truth values.

</div>

### Ways of saying "if p then q"

All of the following mean $p \to q$: "$p$ implies $q$", "$q$ if $p$", "$p$ only if $q$", "$p$ is sufficient for $q$", "$q$ is necessary for $p$", "$q$ whenever $p$".

The one that trips people is **"$p$ only if $q$"**. "You can board only if you have a ticket" means *boarding → ticket*: having a ticket is necessary, not sufficient.

### Converse, inverse and contrapositive

From an implication $p \to q$ we can form three related statements:

| Name | Form | Equivalent to $p \to q$? |
|:-|:-:|:-:|
| Contrapositive | $\lnot q \to \lnot p$ | **Yes** |
| Converse | $q \to p$ | No |
| Inverse | $\lnot p \to \lnot q$ | No (it is equivalent to the converse) |

"If a number is divisible by 4, it is even" is true. Its contrapositive, "if a number is odd, it is not divisible by 4", is also true. Its converse, "if a number is even, it is divisible by 4", is false: 6 is a counterexample. Confusing an implication with its converse is the single most common logical error in arguments, code reviews and specifications.

## Logical equivalence

<div class="box def">

<span class="tag">Definition</span>

Two formulas are **logically equivalent**, written $A \equiv B$, if they have the same truth value in every row of the truth table. A formula that is true in every row is a **tautology**; one that is false in every row is a **contradiction**.

</div>

The equivalences you will use constantly:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p \to q &\equiv \lnot p \lor q && \text{(implication as "or")}\\
p \to q &\equiv \lnot q \to \lnot p && \text{(contrapositive)}\\
\lnot (p \land q) &\equiv \lnot p \lor \lnot q && \text{(De Morgan)}\\
\lnot (p \lor q) &\equiv \lnot p \land \lnot q && \text{(De Morgan)}\\
p \land (q \lor r) &\equiv (p \land q) \lor (p \land r) && \text{(distributive)}\\
\lnot (p \to q) &\equiv p \land \lnot q && \text{(negating an implication)}\\
p \leftrightarrow q &\equiv (p \to q) \land (q \to p)
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice the last-but-one line. The negation of "if it rains, the match is cancelled" is **not** "if it rains, the match is not cancelled". It is "it rains **and** the match is not cancelled". A single situation breaks an implication.

## Predicates and quantifiers

Most interesting statements are about *all* or *some* objects. A **predicate** is an open sentence with variables, like $P(x)$: "$x$ is even". It becomes a proposition when we fix $x$ or **quantify** it.

- **Universal quantifier**: $\forall x \in D,\ P(x)$ means "for every $x$ in the domain $D$, $P(x)$ is true".
- **Existential quantifier**: $\exists x \in D,\ P(x)$ means "there is at least one $x$ in $D$ for which $P(x)$ is true".

The domain matters. $\exists x,\ x^2 = 2$ is false over the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ and true over the reals $\mathbb{R}$.

### Negating quantifiers

$$
\lnot\, \forall x\, P(x) \;\equiv\; \exists x\, \lnot P(x)
\qquad\qquad
\lnot\, \exists x\, P(x) \;\equiv\; \forall x\, \lnot P(x)
$$

To deny "every request gets a response", you do not need to show that no request gets a response. You need **one** request that does not. This is exactly why a single failing test disproves a universal claim, and why passing tests can never prove one.

### Nested quantifiers: order matters

Let $L(x, y)$ mean "$x$ loves $y$".

- $\forall x\, \exists y\, L(x,y)$: everybody loves somebody (possibly different people).
- $\exists y\, \forall x\, L(x,y)$: there is one person whom everybody loves.

The second implies the first, but not the other way round. In mathematics: $\forall n \in \mathbb{N}\ \exists m \in \mathbb{N},\ m > n$ is true (there is no largest natural number), while $\exists m\ \forall n,\ m > n$ is false (no number is larger than all numbers, including itself).

To negate a nested statement, push the $\lnot$ inward one quantifier at a time, flipping each one:

$$
\lnot\, \forall x\, \exists y\, P(x,y) \equiv \exists x\, \lnot \exists y\, P(x,y) \equiv \exists x\, \forall y\, \lnot P(x,y).
$$

<div class="box ex">

<span class="tag">Worked example</span>

**Specification:** "For every user there is an admin who can reset their password."
In symbols: $\forall u\, \exists a,\ \text{CanReset}(a, u)$.

**Its negation**, which is what a bug report would have to show: $\exists u\, \forall a,\ \lnot \text{CanReset}(a, u)$. "There is some user whom no admin can reset."

</div>

## Rules of inference

A valid argument is one where the conclusion must be true whenever all the premises are true. A few patterns do most of the work.

| Rule | Premises | Conclusion |
|:-|:-|:-|
| Modus ponens | $p$, and $p \to q$ | $q$ |
| Modus tollens | $\lnot q$, and $p \to q$ | $\lnot p$ |
| Hypothetical syllogism | $p \to q$, and $q \to r$ | $p \to r$ |
| Disjunctive syllogism | $p \lor q$, and $\lnot p$ | $q$ |
| Universal instantiation | $\forall x\, P(x)$ | $P(c)$ for any particular $c$ |

Two patterns look similar but are **fallacies**:

- **Affirming the consequent**: from $q$ and $p \to q$, concluding $p$. "If the code has a bug, the test fails. The test fails. So the code has a bug." The test itself might be wrong.
- **Denying the antecedent**: from $\lnot p$ and $p \to q$, concluding $\lnot q$.

You can check any argument in the truth-table lab: an argument with premises $P_1, \dots, P_k$ and conclusion $C$ is valid exactly when $(P_1 \land \dots \land P_k) \to C$ is a tautology. Try `((p -> q) & q) -> p` to see the fallacy fail.

## Proof techniques

A **proof** is a sequence of statements, each of which is a premise, a definition, a previously proved result, or follows from earlier statements by a valid rule. Proofs about integers rest on precise definitions, so we start there.

<div class="box def">

<span class="tag">Definitions</span>

An integer $n$ is **even** if $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$, and **odd** if $n = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$. For integers $a \ne 0$ and $b$, **$a$ divides $b$**, written $a \mid b$, if $b = ac$ for some integer $c$.

</div>

Using the definition, not intuition, is the whole game. "It is obvious that an odd number squared is odd" is not a proof; writing $n = 2k+1$ and computing is.

### 1. Direct proof

To prove $p \to q$: assume $p$, then derive $q$.

<div class="box thm">

<span class="tag">Proposition</span>

If $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd.

</div>

*Proof.* Suppose $n$ is odd. Then $n = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$. So
$$n^2 = (2k+1)^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1.$$
Since $2k^2 + 2k$ is an integer, $n^2$ has the form $2m + 1$, so $n^2$ is odd. $\blacksquare$

### 2. Proof by contrapositive

To prove $p \to q$, prove the equivalent $\lnot q \to \lnot p$. Use it when assuming $\lnot q$ gives you something concrete to work with and assuming $p$ does not.

<div class="box thm">

<span class="tag">Proposition</span>

Let $n$ be an integer. If $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.

</div>

A direct proof is awkward: from $n^2 = 2k$ it is not clear how to reach $n$. *Proof (contrapositive).* Suppose $n$ is not even, so $n$ is odd. By the previous proposition, $n^2$ is odd, so $n^2$ is not even. We have shown $\lnot(n \text{ even}) \to \lnot(n^2 \text{ even})$, which is equivalent to the claim. $\blacksquare$

### 3. Proof by contradiction

To prove a statement $S$: assume $\lnot S$ and derive something impossible, a statement of the form $C \land \lnot C$. Since a true assumption cannot lead to a contradiction, $\lnot S$ is false, so $S$ is true.

<div class="box thm">

<span class="tag">Theorem</span>

$\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.

</div>

*Proof.* Suppose, for contradiction, that $\sqrt 2$ is rational. Then $\sqrt 2 = a/b$ for integers $a, b$ with $b \neq 0$, and we may assume the fraction is in lowest terms, so $a$ and $b$ are not both even. Squaring gives $2 = a^2 / b^2$, so $a^2 = 2b^2$. Then $a^2$ is even, so by the previous proposition $a$ is even: $a = 2c$. Substituting, $4c^2 = 2b^2$, so $b^2 = 2c^2$, which makes $b^2$ even and therefore $b$ even. Now $a$ and $b$ are both even, contradicting lowest terms. Hence $\sqrt 2$ is irrational. $\blacksquare$

Notice how the proof reused the proposition we proved by contrapositive. Real proofs are built from smaller proved pieces, exactly like programs are built from tested functions.

### 4. Proof by cases

If the hypothesis splits into cases that cover every possibility, prove the claim in each case.

<div class="box thm">

<span class="tag">Proposition</span>

For every integer $n$, the number $n^2 + n$ is even.

</div>

*Proof.* Every integer is even or odd. **Case 1:** $n = 2k$. Then $n^2 + n = 4k^2 + 2k = 2(2k^2 + k)$, which is even. **Case 2:** $n = 2k+1$. Then $n^2 + n = (2k+1)(2k+2) = 2(2k+1)(k+1)$, which is even. In both cases $n^2 + n$ is even. $\blacksquare$

(A shorter proof: $n^2 + n = n(n+1)$ is a product of two consecutive integers, one of which is even. Finding the short proof usually comes after finding a correct one.)

### 5. If-and-only-if proofs

To prove $p \leftrightarrow q$, prove **both** directions, $p \to q$ and $q \to p$. The two propositions above together prove: *$n$ is even if and only if $n^2$ is even*.

### 6. Existence proofs and counterexamples

To prove $\exists x\, P(x)$, the cleanest way is to exhibit one $x$ and check it (a **constructive** proof). "There is a prime number greater than 100": $101$ is prime, done.

To **disprove** $\forall x\, P(x)$, give one counterexample. "For every positive integer $n$, $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime" holds for $n = 1, 2, \dots, 39$, which might fool you into believing it. But for $n = 40$: $40^2 + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41^2$. Forty successful cases prove nothing; one failure disproves everything.

<div class="box cs">

<span class="tag">In code</span>

This is the logic of testing. A test suite checks $P(x)$ for finitely many $x$. It can **disprove** "the function is correct for all inputs" with one failing case, but it can never prove it. Proofs, types and property-based testing (phase 4) are the tools that reach further.

</div>

## Writing proofs that people can read

A proof is a piece of writing for a skeptical reader. Book of Proof gives guidelines worth following from day one:

1. **State what you are proving and which technique you use.** "We prove the contrapositive." "Suppose, for contradiction, that …"
2. **Start from definitions.** Write out what "even", "divides" or "injective" means in symbols, then work with the symbols.
3. **Write in sentences.** Equations are part of sentences, with words connecting them: "so", "therefore", "since".
4. **Introduce every variable.** "Let $k$ be an integer such that $n = 2k$."
5. **Do not start from what you want to prove** and work towards something true. That proves nothing, because false statements can imply true ones.
6. **End clearly**, with $\blacksquare$ or "This completes the proof."

<div class="box warn">

<span class="tag">Common mistake</span>

"Proof" of $p \to q$ by assuming $q$. For example, to prove "if $n^2$ is even then $n$ is even", writing "let $n = 2k$, then $n^2 = 4k^2$ is even" proves the **converse**, not the statement.

</div>

<div class="exercise">

<span class="tag">Exercise 1</span><span class="lvl-tag">★ basic</span>

Write the contrapositive, converse and inverse of: "If a function has no side effects, then calling it twice gives the same result." Which of them are guaranteed to be true if the original is?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Let $p$ = "the function has no side effects", $q$ = "calling it twice gives the same result".

- Contrapositive ($\lnot q \to \lnot p$): "If calling it twice gives different results, then it has side effects." Equivalent to the original, so true whenever the original is.
- Converse ($q \to p$): "If calling it twice gives the same result, then it has no side effects." Not guaranteed: a function that writes a log line and returns a constant gives the same result twice but has a side effect.
- Inverse ($\lnot p \to \lnot q$): "If it has side effects, then calling it twice gives different results." Not guaranteed, for the same reason. It is equivalent to the converse.

</details>
</div>

<div class="exercise">

<span class="tag">Exercise 2</span><span class="lvl-tag">★ basic</span>

Use a truth table (on paper, then check in the lab) to decide whether $(p \to q) \to r$ and $p \to (q \to r)$ are equivalent.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

They are **not** equivalent. Take $p = $ F, $q = $ F, $r = $ F. Then $p \to q$ is T, so $(p\to q)\to r$ is T → F = **F**. But $p \to (q \to r)$ is F → (anything) = **T**. One differing row is enough. (So implication is not associative; by convention $p \to q \to r$ means $p \to (q \to r)$.)

</details>
</div>

<div class="exercise">

<span class="tag">Exercise 3</span><span class="lvl-tag">★★ core</span>

Write the negation of each statement in symbols and in plain English, with the $\lnot$ pushed all the way in.

(a) $\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ x^2 \ge 0$.
(b) "Every test in the suite passes on some machine."
(c) $\forall \varepsilon > 0\ \exists \delta > 0\ \forall x,\ \bigl(|x| < \delta \to |f(x)| < \varepsilon\bigr)$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

(a) $\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\ x^2 < 0$. "Some real number has a negative square." (The original is true, so this is false.)

(b) Original: $\forall t\, \exists m,\ \text{Pass}(t, m)$. Negation: $\exists t\, \forall m,\ \lnot\text{Pass}(t, m)$. "There is a test that fails on every machine."

(c) $\exists \varepsilon > 0\ \forall \delta > 0\ \exists x,\ \bigl(|x| < \delta \land |f(x)| \ge \varepsilon\bigr)$. Note that the negated implication became an "and". This is how you show a function is **not** continuous at 0.

</details>
</div>

<div class="exercise">

<span class="tag">Exercise 4</span><span class="lvl-tag">★★ core</span>

Is this argument valid? "If the cache is warm, the request is fast. The request is slow. Therefore the cache is cold." Name the rule or the fallacy. Then answer the same question for: "If the cache is warm, the request is fast. The request is fast. Therefore the cache is warm."

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The first is **valid**: from $p \to q$ and $\lnot q$ we conclude $\lnot p$ (modus tollens). The second is **invalid**: it affirms the consequent. The request could be fast for another reason, such as a small payload.

</details>
</div>

<div class="exercise">

<span class="tag">Exercise 5</span><span class="lvl-tag">★★ core</span>

Prove directly: if $a$ and $b$ are odd integers, then $ab$ is odd.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Suppose $a$ and $b$ are odd. Then $a = 2k + 1$ and $b = 2\ell + 1$ for some integers $k, \ell$. So
$ab = (2k+1)(2\ell+1) = 4k\ell + 2k + 2\ell + 1 = 2(2k\ell + k + \ell) + 1.$
Since $2k\ell + k + \ell$ is an integer, $ab$ is odd. $\blacksquare$

</details>
</div>

<div class="exercise">

<span class="tag">Exercise 6</span><span class="lvl-tag">★★ core</span>

Prove: for integers $a, b, c$, if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Suppose $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$. By definition $b = ax$ and $c = by$ for some integers $x, y$. Then $c = (ax)y = a(xy)$. Since $xy$ is an integer, $a \mid c$. $\blacksquare$

</details>
</div>

<div class="exercise">

<span class="tag">Exercise 7</span><span class="lvl-tag">★★ core</span>

Prove by contrapositive: for an integer $n$, if $5n + 3$ is even, then $n$ is odd.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Contrapositive: if $n$ is even, then $5n + 3$ is odd. Suppose $n = 2k$. Then $5n + 3 = 10k + 3 = 2(5k + 1) + 1$, which is odd. Since the contrapositive holds, the original statement holds. $\blacksquare$

</details>

<div class="exercise">

<span class="tag">Exercise 8</span><span class="lvl-tag">★★★ stretch</span>

Prove by contradiction that there is no largest even integer. Then prove by contradiction that $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational. (For the second you will need: if $3 \mid n^2$ then $3 \mid n$. Prove that too, by contrapositive and cases on the remainder of $n$ when divided by 3.)

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**No largest even integer.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $N$ is the largest even integer. Then $N + 2$ is even (since $N = 2k$ gives $N + 2 = 2(k+1)$) and $N + 2 > N$, contradicting that $N$ is largest. $\blacksquare$

**Lemma: if $3 \mid n^2$ then $3 \mid n$.** Contrapositive: if $3 \nmid n$ then $3 \nmid n^2$. If $3 \nmid n$, then $n = 3k+1$ or $n = 3k+2$. Case 1: $n^2 = 9k^2 + 6k + 1 = 3(3k^2+2k) + 1$. Case 2: $n^2 = 9k^2 + 12k + 4 = 3(3k^2 + 4k + 1) + 1$. In both cases $n^2$ leaves remainder 1, so $3 \nmid n^2$. $\blacksquare$

**$\sqrt 3$ is irrational.** Suppose $\sqrt 3 = a/b$ in lowest terms. Then $a^2 = 3b^2$, so $3 \mid a^2$ and by the lemma $3 \mid a$, say $a = 3c$. Then $9c^2 = 3b^2$, so $b^2 = 3c^2$, so $3 \mid b^2$ and $3 \mid b$. Then 3 divides both $a$ and $b$, contradicting lowest terms. $\blacksquare$

</details>
</div>

<div class="exercise">

<span class="tag">Exercise 9</span><span class="lvl-tag">★★★ stretch</span>

Prove or disprove each statement.
(a) For all integers $n$, if $n^2$ is divisible by 4 then $n$ is divisible by 4.
(b) For every integer $n$, $n^2 - n$ is even.
(c) There exist integers $m, n$ with $15m + 25n = 4$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

(a) **False.** Counterexample: $n = 2$. Then $n^2 = 4$ is divisible by 4 but $n$ is not.

(b) **True.** $n^2 - n = n(n-1)$ is a product of two consecutive integers, one of which is even, so the product is even. (Or by cases, as in the $n^2 + n$ proof above.)

(c) **False.** For any integers $m, n$, $15m + 25n = 5(3m + 5n)$ is divisible by 5, but 4 is not. So no such integers exist.

</details>
</div>

<div class="exercise">

<span class="tag">Exercise 10</span><span class="lvl-tag">★★★ stretch</span>

A function `isSorted(xs)` is specified as: for all indices $i$ and $j$, if $i < j$ then `xs[i] <= xs[j]`. Write the specification in symbols, then its negation. What does a minimal "witness" that a list is unsorted look like? Can you simplify the specification to one that only compares neighbours, and prove that the two are equivalent?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Specification: $\forall i\, \forall j,\ (i < j \to x_i \le x_j)$. Negation: $\exists i\, \exists j,\ (i < j \land x_i > x_j)$. A witness is one pair of positions $i < j$ that is out of order.

Neighbour form: $\forall i,\ x_i \le x_{i+1}$ (for valid $i$). The full form implies the neighbour form (take $j = i + 1$). Conversely, if neighbours are ordered and $i < j$, then $x_i \le x_{i+1} \le \dots \le x_j$, so $x_i \le x_j$ by transitivity of $\le$. Strictly, that "…" is an induction on $j - i$, which is the subject of lesson 1.2.3. This is why `isSorted` only needs one pass over the list.

</details>
</div>

## Summary

- A proposition is true or false. Connectives build compound propositions; implication is false only when the hypothesis is true and the conclusion false.
- An implication is equivalent to its contrapositive, not to its converse.
- Negation swaps $\forall$ and $\exists$, and turns $p \to q$ into $p \land \lnot q$.
- The order of nested quantifiers changes the meaning.
- Five techniques cover most proofs: direct, contrapositive, contradiction, cases, and construction or counterexample.
- Start from definitions, write in sentences, and never assume what you want to prove.
